Design Strategy for a Generative E‑Ink
Visualization Engine
Overview and Goals
This project envisions an ambient, generative art display driven by smart home data. Unlike a traditional
dashboard, the goal is to produce a  “living” grayscale artwork on a Waveshare 16-level e‑ink panel,
reflecting home activity in subtle, poetic ways. The system must consolidate hundreds of Home Assistant
(HA)  sensor  entities  into  a  few  semantic  input  channels (e.g.  house  activity,  soundscape,  resource  use, 
climate  comfort)  that  drive  the  visuals.  It  should  be  modular  and  extensible –  new  devices  or  visual
mappings can be added via configuration rather than code – and optimized for e‑ink’s unique performance
characteristics (partial refresh, low refresh rate, ghosting, dithering, etc.). We will outline a comprehensive
architecture, from data ingestion to rendering, and discuss best practices (including known community
tricks) to balance hardware limitations with artistic richness.
Mapping Home Data to Semantic Channels
Instead  of  mapping  each  sensor  directly  to  a  visual  element  (which  would  create  an  overload  of
information), the engine uses  normalized “proxy” measures that aggregate and abstract the raw data.
This follows the strategy of systems like Tableau Machine, which achieved an enchanting ambient display by
“mapping computed proxy measures to the design attributes of the 2D composition, rather than to individual
[sensor]  elements”,  thereby  encouraging  interpretation  without  one-to-one  literalness.  Direct,  literal
mappings (like showing every sensor reading) feel like surveillance or rote regurgitation, while completely
random visuals are unintelligible; a midpoint of data-driven but ambiguous representation sustains long-
term interest . In practice, this means defining a handful of semantic channels that each combine
multiple related sensors into a single numeric or categorical value which influences the artwork.
Proposed Channels and Inputs:
- House Activity: A composite measure of occupancy and motion. It could synthesize motion sensors (Hue,
Aqara  PIRs),  entry  sensors (doors/locks,  garage  door  openers),  and  presence  (e.g.  Eero  router  device
presence or phone trackers) into an “activity level” metric. For example, each motion event or door unlock
might spike this channel’s value (with a decay over time), producing a higher “energy” when the home is
busy. Even aggregate stats like number of rooms active or frequency of recent events can feed this channel.
- Soundscape: Represents ambient sound or music activity. This might tap into Apple HomePod status (e.g.
whether music is playing and at what volume) or any sound level sensors available. A HomePod integrated
as a media_player in HA can indicate if audio is playing; multiple active speakers or higher volume could
increase the soundscape intensity. (If actual dB noise sensors were present, they would contribute as well.)
-  Resource Use: Abstracts the consumption of resources like water , energy or network. For instance, the
Flume water monitor reporting current water flow or daily usage can map to this channel – high water
flow might raise a “water usage” sub-component. If energy monitoring is available (not listed, but possibly
via smart plugs or the power draw of devices on Eero), that could factor in too. In absence of direct power
sensors, we might use proxies like the number of devices online (from Eero) or HVAC usage from the

-  Climate Comfort: An indicator of environmental comfort inside the home. This would draw on  Ecobee

uncomfortable (too hot/cold/humid) and 100% = optimal comfort. 
Each channel’s value is computed by a  normalization module that translates diverse sensor units into a
unified 0–1 or low–high scale. Configuration files (e.g. YAML/JSON) will define these mappings. For example,
a YAML config might list each relevant HA entity and how it affects a channel: 
house_activity: include binary_sensor.living_room_motion (weight 1.0 on motion event), 
sensor.front_door_lock (trigger burst when unlock), etc., with parameters like decay rates for
activity. 
soundscape: link media_player.kitchen_homepod.volume (map 0–1 volume to channel) and 
media_player.living_room_homepod.state (add fixed value if playing). 
resource_use: link sensor.flume_water_gpm (map 0–10 GPM flow to 0–1 scale) and perhaps 
sensor.internet_bandwidth (normalized) or sensor.thermostat_hvac_power if available. 
climate_comfort: link sensor.living_room_temperature (with a target comfort range) and 
sensor.living_room_humidity, as well as climate.home_thermostat.hvac_action (if
heating/cooling is running, comfort is not ideal). 
This config-driven approach makes the system extensible – adding a new device simply means updating
the  config  to  include  it  in  the  appropriate  channel.  Internally,  the  normalization  logic  can  be  generic,
applying simple formulae (like linear scaling, thresholds, or event triggers) as specified in the config. 
Notably, these channels are designed to be semantically meaningful but not overly explicit. The display will not
show raw sensor values or icons for each device; instead it will use these channels to influence an abstract
visual. This ensures the output remains  artistic and ambient. Home inhabitants should be able to sense
patterns (e.g. the display grows more animated when many people are active, or a gentle visual quiet when
the house is still at night) without reading numbers. Prior research in ambient displays shows that such
abstract yet responsive visuals can increase awareness while  “not exposing specific values” or demanding
focused attention .

Home Assistant Integration (Data Ingestion)
To feed real-time data into the visualization engine, we leverage Home Assistant’s integration capabilities.
Two reliable patterns can be used here:
MQTT Event Stream: Home Assistant’s built-in MQTT integration (specifically the MQTT Statestream)
can publish state changes to an MQTT broker on your network. This integration can be configured to
“publish  state  changes  in  Home  Assistant  to  individual  MQTT  topics” for  selected  entities.  For
example, when a motion sensor changes state or a new temperature reading is available, HA will
publish a message to a topic like home/<entity_id>/state. The visualization engine acts as an
MQTT client (using a library such as paho-mqtt in Python or an MQTT addon in C++) subscribing to
the relevant topics. This decouples the system nicely: Home Assistant just announces updates, and
the engine reacts to them asynchronously. The MQTT broker (e.g. Mosquitto) could run on the Home
Assistant host or separately. Using MQTT is advantageous for a modular system, since adding a new
device just means subscribing to one more topic, and even remote or low-power devices (ESPHome
sensors, etc.) already speak MQTT. 
WebSocket API: Alternatively, the engine can interface with Home Assistant’s WebSocket API, which
“can be used to stream information from a Home Assistant instance to any client”. The engine would
connect to HA’s WebSocket endpoint (authenticated via a long-lived access token) and subscribe to
event types or state_changed events for entities of interest. This provides real-time push updates
without  an  external  broker .  There  are  Python  client  libraries  (and  examples  using  asyncio
websockets  or  the  homeassistant Python  package)  that  make  subscribing  to  HA  events

Config-driven Mapping: The first component inside our engine is the Semantic Channel Normalization
module  (see  diagram)  which  processes  incoming  events.  This  module  uses  the  user-defined  config
mappings to decide how each HA entity’s data affects the channels. For instance, if a message comes in for
sensor.kitchen_motion =  on,  the  module  sees  from  config  that  this  adds,  say,  +0.3  to  the
house_activity channel (which then decays gradually). A thermostat temperature update might be compared
against the comfort range and update the climate_comfort metric. The normalization module maintains the
current value/state of each semantic channel in memory. It may also apply smoothing or rate limiting – e.g.
averaging  readings  over  a  short  window,  or  ensuring  abrupt  spikes  are  toned  down  –  to  prevent  the
visualization from jittering with every tiny sensor fluctuation. Each channel value is thus a continuously
updated, normalized signal. 
This design makes it easy to add or remove data sources: the config can include an include list of entity IDs
for each channel (or conversely an exclude for irrelevant ones). Because the system handles many entities,
it’s wise to filter to only those needed – the MQTT Statestream can be set up to publish only selected entities
to reduce noise. (Alternatively, one could run a lightweight filter in the engine to ignore topics not in
config.)
Visualization Engine Core
The heart of the system is the Generative Visualization Engine. It takes the semantic channel values and
produces an image (frame) for the e-ink display. The engine is designed in a layered, modular fashion to
maximize creativity and extensibility:
Layered Visual Synthesis: Each semantic channel (or combination of channels) controls one aspect
or “layer” of the visual composition. Instead of drawing one monolithic image in one go, the engine
can maintain multiple layers (e.g. backgrounds, foreground elements, overlays) that correspond to
different data-driven effects. For example, a background layer might represent climate comfort as a
slowly morphing texture or gradient: if the house is comfortable, it could render a calm, uniform
pattern; if discomfort rises (too hot/cold), perhaps a noisy or fractal pattern subtly appears, like
visual “heatwaves” or “chill” noise. A motion/activity layer could consist of particles or abstract shapes
that are generated when motion events occur – e.g. a dot or ripple emanating from a position that
might correspond to a room, or simply an increase in the number of floating shapes on the canvas
when activity is high. A  sound layer might visualize music playing as gentle pulsating rings or a
waveform stripe that animates with the beat or volume (since e-ink is slow, this would not be real-
time audio waveform, but a slowly updating pattern, perhaps a bar that grows with volume or circles
that expand when sound is on). A resource use layer could introduce geometric patterns (like water
ripple concentric circles or pixelated clouds) whose density or size correlates with water flow or
energy use. All layers are then composited to form the final frame. Composition could be simply
drawing them on top of each other with appropriate transparency or blending (in 16 shades of gray)

Generative  Algorithms: Emphasis  is  on  procedural  generation rather  than  fixed  images.
Techniques  from  creative  coding  can  be  employed:  noise  functions  (Perlin/Simplex  noise)  to
generate organic textures that can be modulated by sensor values, particle systems whose behavior
rules (speed, count, spawn rate) are driven by channel intensities, L-systems or abstract drawings
that evolve based on inputs, etc. The engine should allow plugging in new algorithms easily. For
modularity, one can structure the code so that each channel or layer is handled by a separate class
or module (e.g. a “ParticleLayer” for activity, a “NoiseBackgroundLayer” for climate, etc.), each with a
function to update/draw itself given the current channel value. Adding a new mapping or visual
effect then means adding a new layer module and referencing it in a config. 
Expressiveness  over  Precision: Because  this  is  an  art  display,  the  visual  mapping  can  be
metaphorical. For instance,  house activity might correspond to the  movement of elements (lots of
motion = faster or more chaotic movement on screen), while  soundscape might correspond to a
rhythmic pulsing or pattern change (music playing triggers a faint oscillating border or graphical
“equalizer” bar in the composition). Resource use could alter the brightness or texture – e.g. heavy
resource  consumption  might  add  a  granular  texture  or  darker  tint  to  the  image  (a  subtle  cue
analogous to “stress” on the environment). These choices are artistic; the key is that over time users
can intuitively correlate them (e.g. “The background turns speckled when we’re using a lot of water or
power” or  “When the house is busy, these shapes start dancing around”). User studies of ambient art
have found that people will  interpret the display in terms of their activities, even if the system isn’t
explicitly literal – which is exactly the goal. For example, they might say “the display is calm
because we’re quiet” or joke that the system gets “unstable” if one group of occupants doesn’t do
chores , indicating the display successfully prompts reflection without showing any direct text
or alerts.
Aesthetic Cohesion: All layers should be designed to form a cohesive visual style (e.g. a minimalistic
grayscale palette, possibly inspired by nature or abstract art). Since the display is 16-level grayscale,
we can use subtle shading and patterns. The design should avoid a cluttered look; negative (white)
space is valuable on e-ink to maintain a calm appearance. It may help to impose an overall visual
theme (for instance, imagining the display as a kind of generative “landscape” of the home’s state –
calm skies vs. noisy clouds, etc., metaphorically). The architecture supports this by allowing a custom
rendering function for each layer that can incorporate any logic. 
From a technical implementation standpoint, there are a few options to develop this engine: 
Python-based Approach: Python is convenient for tying into Home Assistant (via MQTT or API) and
for reading config files. Libraries like  Pillow (PIL) or  Pycairo can be used to draw shapes, text, or
images onto an in-memory canvas (an image buffer). Pillow supports basic drawing (points, lines,
text) and composition of images; this could suffice for simple generative graphics or for rendering
precomputed patterns. However , complex real-time generative art (many particles, fluid noise) in
Python might be slow, especially on a Raspberry Pi. Python can still drive the high-level logic and
possibly call out to faster libraries (NumPy for pixel operations, or even OpenCV for certain image
filters). One approach is to prototype visuals in Python (for ease of development) and optimize
hotspots with libraries or by moving to C/C++ if needed.

openFrameworks  (C++): The  prompt  suggests  using  openFrameworks,  which  is  a  C++  creative

(including Raspberry Pi) and on macOS, making it a good choice for cross-platform deployment. We
can build the visualization engine as an openFrameworks app that subscribes to MQTT (there are
addons for MQTT or one can use C++ MQTT client libraries) and then use openFrameworks’ drawing
API  to  render  to  an  image  or  directly  to  the  screen.  The  advantage of  openFrameworks  is
performance – it can handle more sprites, higher resolution noise, etc., at the cost of more complex
development. Since e-ink is not a high frame-rate display, we don’t need video-like 60fps rendering,
but we might want to smoothly animate something at e.g. 1–2fps or update small parts frequently,
which C++ can handle more efficiently. The openFrameworks app could either draw into an offscreen
buffer and send that to the e-ink driver , or possibly use a Linux framebuffer driver for the e-ink (if
available) so that drawing calls go directly to the e-ink as if it were a window. (On a Raspberry Pi,
Waveshare e-ink HATs typically interface via SPI, not as a standard monitor , so we’d likely need to use
Waveshare’s library or our own SPI code. We can still have openFrameworks generate the pixel data,
then call a function to push it to SPI.)
Hybrid: It’s  possible  to  use  Python  for  data  +  config  management  and  offload  graphics  to  a
specialized  process.  For  example,  the  Python  side  could  send  high-level  drawing  commands  or
parameters to a separate process (maybe even an ESP32 or another microcontroller that drives the
e-ink). However , given the desire for rich generative visuals, it’s more straightforward that the same
application handles both data and drawing. A clean modular approach might be to separate the
concerns: a Python service could collect/normalize HA data and publish a small set of “visual state”
messages  (via  MQTT  or  shared  memory) that  a  visualization  program  (in  openFrameworks  or
Processing, etc.) subscribes to. This way, the visuals code doesn’t need to have HA integration built-
in; it only needs to receive the already-processed channel values. This decoupling means the visuals
module could be swapped out or run on a different machine. For instance, the Mac mini (M4) could
run the heavy visualization and then send frames to the Raspberry Pi attached to the e-ink. In fact,
one  proven  pattern  (used  in  some  DIY  e-ink  art  projects)  is  to  generate  images  on  a  powerful
machine  and  send  them  to  a  lightweight  display  driver:  “We  built  an  e-ink  frame  that  shows  AI-
generated art by generating images on a desktop and serving them to ESP32/ Raspberry Pi frames via a
local  server” .  In  our  case,  the  Mac  mini  could  run  the  generative  art  software  (taking
advantage of its GPU for any rendering, if needed), and the Raspberry Pi could be a thin client that
simply receives a new image (e.g. via MQTT’s binary payload or an HTTP request) and writes it to the
e-ink. This is probably overkill for simpler graphics, but it’s an option if the visuals become very
computational  (or  if  one  wants  to  leverage  Mac-specific  tools  like  Core  Graphics  or  Metal  for
rendering). On the other hand, the Raspberry Pi (especially a Pi 4 or Pi 5) is capable enough to run
moderately complex openFrameworks sketches on its own, so it may be simpler to run everything on
the Pi for a self-contained device attached to the e-ink panel.

Driving the E-Ink Display
Waveshare’s 16-level grayscale e-ink display will be the canvas for our visuals. These e-paper displays have
very different characteristics from typical screens, so careful consideration is needed in the rendering
pipeline to achieve good performance and longevity:
Refresh  Modes: E-ink  displays  have  multiple  update  modes  with  trade-offs  between  speed,
ghosting, and available grayscale levels. According to documentation for similar Waveshare models,
there is usually a full refresh (slow, ~2–5 seconds, but clears ghosting with a complete black/white
flash) and a partial refresh mode (much faster , ~0.3–0.5 seconds for a region or the whole screen,
with no full-screen blink) . Full refresh allows all 16 grayscale levels with optimal contrast,
whereas partial refresh often works in a bilevel (black/white) mode or with very limited grayscale
(some controllers support 1–2 additional gray levels in partial mode). Our strategy will be to use
partial refreshes for most incremental updates (to avoid the distracting flicker) and occasionally
trigger a full refresh to  reset the screen and clear any ghosting. This mirrors how e-
readers operate (they only do a full flash every few page turns). One community member describes
using  “partial refresh… with no flickering at all” for quick updates, combined with a scheduled full
update every N cycles to erase afterimages. For example, we might do quick partial updates for
minor visual changes, a faster “single flicker” update every 10 minutes, and a full complete refresh
once an hour. The exact schedule can be tuned by observing ghosting on the specific panel, but
the principle (partial most of the time, full occasionally) is well-established.
Partial Update Regions: Importantly, partial mode allows updating only a section of the display.
We should exploit this by limiting refresh to the regions that actually change. For instance, if a small

one region from others (some ghosting or voltage bleed can occur just outside the updated area).
It’s a good practice to occasionally refresh slightly beyond the minimal region or overlap regions a
bit to avoid accumulating edge artifacts.
Avoiding Ghosting:Ghosting refers to faint remnants of previous images. It’s a known issue when
doing repeated partial updates without fully resetting pixels. Over time, contrast can degrade or past
text leaves a slight echo. To mitigate ghosting: 
Periodic full refresh: as mentioned, will wipe the slate. The driver datasheet often recommends a
full refresh at least every few hundred partial updates. For example, one tutorial notes: “After
several partial refreshing operations, a full refresh should be performed to remove the residual image.
Otherwise the residual image may become permanent.”. We will implement a timer or counter so
that, say, every 100 partial updates or every 10 minutes (whichever sooner) triggers a full clear . This
can be done during a moment where the disruption is acceptable (perhaps when house is empty or
at a certain time of day, we briefly blank and refresh the screen). 
Ghosting-aware visuals: We can design the visuals themselves to be  ghost-tolerant. High-contrast
edges tend to leave more ghost; large areas that flip from black to white and back are noticeable.
Using dithered patterns or mid-level grays can actually mask ghosting, as the eye is less sensitive to
faint ghost in a textured gray versus a white background. In other words, a bit of intentional noise
can  make  ghosting  less  obvious.  Some  community  projects  even  use  ghosting  creatively  –  for
instance, updating text in the same location with partial mode can leave a “motion blur” effect of the
old text, which could be an aesthetic choice if done carefully (though we likely don’t want that here).
Nonetheless, keeping most of the display in moderate gray tones and avoiding rapidly inverting
large regions will naturally minimize ghosting. If a part of the image needs to frequently change
from  black  to  white,  consider  cycling  it  through  intermediate  grays  or  patterns instead  of  a  hard
inversion;  this  gives  the  particles  in  the  e-ink  more  chance  to  settle  and  reduces  abrupt  ghost
outlines . 
Manual ghost clearing: As an intermediate between full flashes, some have used techniques like
local flashing – e.g. toggling a region to black then white quickly to clear it. The Waveshare controller
LUT can sometimes be customized to drive a  “clean” for just a part of the screen. If ghosting is
observed in a specific area that updates often (say an area where a shape is oscillating), the engine
could occasionally force that area to blink (perhaps during the scheduled refresh interval). We will
rely on the documented method (full refresh) primarily, but it’s good to know these tricks exist. 
Partial Update Performance: Partial refreshes on modern e-ink can be quite fast – on the order of a
few hundred milliseconds for a small region. In practice, users have achieved ~3–4 updates per
second on some black/white e-ink panels using partial mode. Our 16-level grayscale panel might
be a bit slower , but since we likely use partial in bilevel mode, we can expect maybe 0.5s or faster

Grayscale and Dithering: The target panel supports 16 grayscale levels (4-bit). However , in partial
mode,  true  grayscale  updates  might  either  be  unsupported  or  prone  to  artifacts.  A  known
approach is to use dithering to approximate grays in partial updates. Dithering is the technique of
mixing black and white pixels to create the illusion of intermediate shade. If we find that partial
mode  only  does  B/W,  we  will  convert  our  intended  grayscale  output  to  a  binary  bitmap  with
dithering whenever doing a partial update. The engine can maintain a full grayscale image internally,
but use an error-diffusion algorithm when sending it to the display. The most common is  Floyd–
Steinberg dithering, which distributes quantization error to neighboring pixels, yielding a pleasing
pseudo-gray. However , Floyd–Steinberg can produce a lot of high-frequency noise. An alternative
known to work well on e-ink is Atkinson dithering, which diffuses a smaller portion of the error and
tends  to  create  a  cleaner  result  (fewer  “checkerboard”  artifacts).  In  one  e-ink  art  project,
developers found “Atkinson dithering works much better for low-resolution images… avoiding harsh B/W
patterns and leading to a cleaner, more visually pleasing result”. We will likely adopt Atkinson or a
similar error diffusion method when converting any grayscale buffers to 1-bit for partial updates.
The Python implementation can be accelerated (the mentioned project used Numba to JIT compile
the inner loops of the dithering routine). If our panel (or a library like the UC8151 MicroPython
driver by antirez ) allows partial refreshing with more grayscale, we could try that too. In fact, the
community  has  seen  innovations  like  a  driver  that  “displays  greyscale  images  without  relying  on
dithering” and an “anti-flicker refresh mode” for e-ink . That particular example achieved 32 levels of
gray on a UC8151-based display by carefully tuning the waveform LUT, avoiding the classic flicker (at
the cost of slower updates and requiring occasional full refresh to clear ghosting). It’s a reminder
that the e-ink controller is quite configurable – advanced users have custom-tailored waveforms to
get  fast  partial  updates  and  more  shades  (even  riskily  overclocking  or  using  undocumented
commands). For our prototype, we can stick to vendor-supported modes and dithering, but keep
in mind that such “easter eggs” exist – e.g. one could experiment with using a medium waveform to
update with 4 or 16 grays and see if ghosting is acceptable. 
Layered Composition on E-ink: Since we plan multiple visual layers, an important implementation
detail is how to compose them for output. We have two choices: (a) flatten all layers into one final
image and do a single partial update (covering the bounding box of all changes), or (b) update each
layer’s region separately. Option (a) is simpler and ensures we maintain correct overdrawing (some
e-ink controllers do not support alpha blending natively, so it must be done in software). We would
maintain a full frame buffer (at 16 levels) that is the composite of all layers at the current time. Each
time  step,  we  compare  this  new  frame  to  the  previous  frame  to  determine  what  changed.  For
changed regions, if small, we partial refresh them; if a large portion changed or if the composition as
a whole has drifted significantly, we might refresh a larger region or the whole screen (perhaps
using the fast mode with one flash if needed). The system might also intelligently separate static
from dynamic content: e.g. a static background that only changes when climate comfort significantly
shifts (maybe once an hour) can be drawn and held, and dynamic overlays (particles, etc.) drawn on

In summary, the e-ink driver subsystem will be responsible for: receiving new frame (or region) pixel data
from the visualization engine, sending the proper commands for partial or full update, and managing
timing (not issuing updates too fast for the panel’s limits). We will use the recommended commands from
Waveshare’s library or the GoodDisplay controller datasheet. The engine should also handle the case of the
display sleeping: e-ink panels often have a deep sleep mode to conserve power when idle. If our device is
plugged  in  (e.g.  a  powered  display  on  a  wall),  we  might  not  need  to  sleep  frequently  –  maybe  only
overnight. But if we do put the panel to sleep between updates, we must be cautious because, as one
developer  discovered,  the  controller’s  RAM  loses  data  when  powered  down,  which  can  corrupt  partial
updates if you try to do them immediately after waking. The fix was to either avoid sleeping when
using frequent partials, or to perform an initial full refresh after wake so the controller’s memory matches
the actual screen before attempting partial updates. We will incorporate that knowledge: if the system
has a long gap with no updates and we put the screen to sleep (to save power or lifespan), then on the next
update we will do a full or fast refresh cycle to resynchronize, then resume partials.
Deployment Options: Raspberry Pi vs Mac Mini
The architecture is intended to be platform-agnostic. Both the Mac mini (with an Apple M4 or similar) and a
Raspberry Pi can run the core engine. The choice may depend on where the e-ink display is attached and
how much local processing we want:
Raspberry Pi Deployment: The Waveshare e-ink 16-gray display typically comes as a HAT or SPI
module that interfaces readily with a Raspberry Pi’s GPIO pins. A Pi (e.g. a Pi 4 Model B or Pi Zero 2
W) can thus directly drive the panel using Waveshare’s Python library or spidev. The entire stack –
MQTT client, data normalization, and visual generation – can run on the Pi. We might use Python for
everything on the Pi for simplicity, or compile a C++ openFrameworks app for the Pi if performance
demands. The Pi can be small and mounted with the display in a frame, making a self-contained
ambient device. One successful project, HomePlate (Inkplate 10), followed this model by running
an ESPHome or Pi-based app to display HA data on an e-ink screen (though HomePlate was
more static dashboard). Our project, focusing on creative visuals, would similarly benefit from local
execution on Pi to reduce latency and dependency on a separate machine.
Mac Mini Deployment: If the Mac mini is readily available and perhaps co-located with the display
(or able to communicate with it), it offers much more computing power . The Mac could run the
visualization  engine  with  heavy  algorithms  or  even  incorporate  on-device  AI  (for  instance,
theoretically using a neural network to generate art – outside our scope, but feasible given the
mention of AI in some contexts). The challenge is connecting the e-ink display: The Waveshare SPI
HAT  cannot  plug  directly  into  a  Mac,  but  we  could  use  a  USB-to-SPI  adapter  or ,  easier ,  use  a
Raspberry Pi or microcontroller as a  bridge. For example, the Mac could send high-level drawing
commands or entire frame images to a microcontroller (ESP32 or Pi) that physically drives the e-ink.
This is essentially the architecture used in that AI e-ink art frame project, where they hosted a “picture
server” on Home Assistant/RPi and had ESP32 frames fetch images. In our case, we could set
up a lightweight HTTP or MQTT image transport: the Mac generates a PNG image of the artwork and
posts it to the Pi (which then displays it). Latency isn’t a huge concern if this is done once every few

To implement this system successfully, we can draw on knowledge and code from similar projects and

E-Ink Driver Strategies: The Waveshare display likely uses a driver IC (such as an IT8951 or IL0373
for  grayscale)  for  which  Waveshare  provides  sample  code.  It’s  worth  reviewing  the  official
Waveshare e-Paper Python library and the controller datasheet to understand supported update
modes.  For  partial  updates,  resources  like  the  Makerguides  tutorial  “Partial  Refresh  of  e-Paper
Display”  provide  sample  code  and  explain  how  to  use  functions  like
setPartialWindow(x,y,w,h) in popular e-ink libraries (like GxEPD for Arduino). They also
demonstrate combining partial refresh with deep sleep and multiple regions, which we plan to
emulate . The blog post “Fixing Waveshare e-paper partial updates” by Hugo*/2025 details the
discovery of the RAM volatility issue on partial refresh after delays, which guided our decision to do a full
refresh after long sleeps . For low-level tuning, Ben Krasnow’s work on fast LUTs is illuminating: he
managed to “double the refresh rate” on a 4.2″ panel by modifying waveform tables, at the cost of some
burn-in  after  thousands  of  cycles.  While  we  may  not  hack  the  LUT  in  our  first  prototype,
understanding that the LUT controls ghosting vs. speed trade-off is useful if we need to fine-tune (the
Hackaday article on [antirez]’s driver highlights that “controller chips are highly configurable… it’s possible
to do much more than the approved modes (with risk)”). Another nifty idea from Kindle devices: they
sometimes refresh via an “animated wipe” (line by line) to reduce perceptible flicker – while we
won’t implement that from scratch, it’s interesting to note how the user experience can be improved
by non-traditional refresh patterns.

Community  Optimizations  (“Easter  Eggs”): We  will  include  notes  on  any  lesser-known  tricks
discovered by others. For example: setting the e-ink panel’s  VCOM voltage optimally can reduce
ghosting (some Waveshare displays allow adjusting this in software or via a trimmer – too high or

ghost images longer). The community has found that turning off the display power or using deep
sleep when idle can greatly extend life and minimize ghosting; one source notes “after a refresh, it’s
recommended to power off or hibernate the display to prolong its life” – so when the house is idle
overnight, we might put the panel to sleep and wake it in the morning with a fresh screen. There’s
also a fun hack: partial refresh for animation – some have managed to do rudimentary animations
on e-ink (like the “SlowMovie” project that plays a movie at one frame per several seconds). The trick
is often to update just parts of the image in sequence to give the illusion of motion without flicker .
We can borrow those ideas for any subtle animations (like perhaps “wiping” the screen with a new
pattern  slowly  from  one  side  to  the  other ,  which  could  be  less  jarring  than  a  full  flash).  If  we
incorporate any of these, we’ll reference the source (e.g. Ben Krasnow’s partial refresh demo video,
or hackaday discussions where someone mentions  “higher-end Kindles animate refresh from left to
right” ).

In conclusion, this design balances on the line between hardware constraints and artistic expression. By
simplifying input data into a few meaningful signals and using those to drive a thoughtfully designed
generative graphic, we create a display that is aware of the home without being a literal dashboard. The use
of e-ink lends itself to this application: its paper-like quality and slow update nature encourage calm, slow-
changing  visuals  that  can  be  glanced  at  throughout  the  day.  With  careful  use  of  partial  updates  and
dithering, we can achieve surprisingly dynamic effects on e-ink (as hackers have shown, even things like
continuous clocks or animated images are possible). And by building the system to be modular , we
ensure it can grow – whether that means adding new sensor inputs (say a future air quality sensor channel)
or swapping in a new visual theme (since an artist might want to re-skin the visualization entirely, e.g.
change from abstract geometric to organic). The end result will be an ambient home visualization frame
that runs quietly, refreshes itself smartly to avoid burn-in, and gives the household a gentle artistic mirror
of their living environment.
